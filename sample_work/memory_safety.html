Swatting the Billion Dollar Bug

The Search for Memory Safety

A Tale of Three Cyberattacks

On Saturday, January 25, 2003, internet users started having difficulty connecting to websites. 20% of data was failing to reach its destination, and some banking and travel sites were completely unreachable. South Korea was particularly badly affected, with its internet almost completely shutting down. The cause: SQL Slammer, a brand-new virus infecting Microsoft database software, which spewed malicious requests over the internet as fast as it could, doubling the number of infections every 8.5 seconds until it controlled over 250,000 computers (Cisco Systems 2003). Luckily, the Slammer's programmer did not include any code to erase files on or otherwise damage the computers that it infected, and its attacks were easily distinguishable from normal Internet traffic, so the internet recovered within hours. (Moore et al 2003) Nevertheless, the worm caused major disruption and was a sign of things to come.

On Friday, the 12th of May 2017, over 200,000 people around the world turned on their computers to see a strange message: “Oops, your files have been encrypted!” In just one day, a new ransomware virus known as WannaCry had become a digital pandemic. (BBC 2017) It infected 34 hospitals run by the British National Health Service, disabling medical devices like MRI machines, forcing the NHS to cancel 13,500 appointments and redirect some patients to other hospitals. (Ghafur et al. 2019) Gas stations in the Chinese city of Chongqing had to take all payments in cash as systems at the national oil company were taken down, while several Renault auto factories stopped production. (BBC 2017) Fortunately, that evening a British security researcher managed to activate a built-in “kill switch” in the malware and stop its spread, but in just one day it had already caused an estimated $4 billion in damage. (Berr 2017)

Over a period of time from the mid-2010s to the present day, journalists and human rights activists in many countries received suspicious text messages, including a link leading to a seemingly blank web page. Investigations by the University of Toronto’s Citizen Lab staff found those web pages belong to NSO, an Israeli “cyber warfare” company, and were intended to infect their phones with Pegasus, spyware that the company sells to governments ostensibly for catching lawbreakers. Pegasus can peruse all the data on a target’s phone, snoop on their conversations, track their location, and even listen to their daily life using the device’s microphone. (Scott-Railton et al 2017a) Targets include staff at the Mexican media outlet Aristegui Noticias, which was investigating a bribery scandal where a construction company bought a mansion for the President’s wife, Carlos Loret de Mola, a Mexican journalist reporting on extrajudicial killings by the country’s security forces, Ahmed Mansoor, a pro-democracy activist and Martin Ennals human rights award winner in the UAE, and several Palestinian human rights activists. (Scott-Railton et al 2017a) (Scott-Railton et. al 2017b)

These three viruses, SQL Slammer, WannaCry, and Pegasus, have many differences. They infect different pieces of software-- SQL Server, Windows, and iOS-- and serve different purposes-- mischief, extortion, and espionage. Yet they all rely on the same kind of vulnerability, a set of two mistakes that programmers have repeated over and over again since the beginning of their profession, a friend of hackers since 1988: memory unsafety bugs.

These bugs have plagued the computer industry for years, but they may not plague it forever. A move towards new programming languages, encouraged by the US government, may get rid of them once and for all.

What is Memory Safety?

Whenever a computer program receives a collection of data, whether it is an e-mail, an internet request from another computer, or a cat photo, it must store that data at some location in its memory, making it a “memory object.” Computer programs use variables (or small pieces of data) called references to keep track of the location of memory objects, so that they can access them. Computer memory is broken into a series of numbered slots containing 8 bits of data, enough for one character of the Latin alphabet. A reference contains the number of the slot containing the first 8 bits of the memory object’s data, and the program can keep moving from there to the next and next slot until it has read the entire object.

A program is memory safe if, when a program uses a reference to an object, it always accesses the correct object. By contrast a memory UNsafety bug occurs when a program uses a reference to one object but ends up reading or writing to a different object. This can happen for two reasons. With spatial memory unsafety bugs, also known as buffer overflows, a program moves past the end of the memory object that it is trying to access and ends up reading or writing to whatever objects happen to be located after it in memory. This can happen, for instance, if the program reads or writes to a list of items and does not include a check to see if it has reached the end of the list. Temporal memory unsafety bugs, also known as use-after-free bugs, occur when the object that the program is trying to access has been deleted from memory, and a different object has been created in its place. (Internet Security Research Group n. d)

Although these bugs are relatively simple, they can occur in almost any part of a program, making them difficult to eliminate, so they are very common. The first virus to exploit one of these bugs was the 1988 Morris Worm, which used a buffer overflow vulnerability to infect 10% of computers on the (very small at the time) internet and cause up to $10 million dollars in damage (Tkacik 2021). They continue to be a problem today: 44% of known exploited vulnerabilities in the Linux kernel, 70% of the vulnerabilities in Microsoft products in 2018 (MSRC Team 2019), and 80% of those in Google’s Android Operating System in 2019 (Vander Stroep & Chong 2019) are memory safety bugs.

Can Memory Unsafety Be Prevented?

Yes! Many programming languages prevent all memory unsafety vulnerabilities in code. The first such programming language, LISP, was created in 1960. Spatial memory safety can be assured by simply inserting code that checks the size of a data structure before reading it, but temporal safety is more complicated. To prevent temporal memory safety errors, the language used a strategy called “Garbage Collection.” With garbage collection, the programmer never has to explicitly write code to delete unused objects from memory; instead the program periodically runs code to find and remove them. This code scans through the program’s references, starting from certain base references, then scanning the references in memory objects that these references point to and the references in objects pointed to by those references, until it has scanned all the references in the program. Whenever it finds a reference to a specific memory object it marks that memory object as in use. If there are no remaining references pointing to an object then the program cannot use that object and so it is deleted. (McCarthy et al 1960) Because memory objects are only deleted when there are no references to them, the program will never try to use a reference to an object that has been deleted, so use-after-free vulnerabilities will never happen.

However, early garbage collectors had a large performance cost. The 1960 LISP paper said that each run of the collector took “several seconds” (a near-eternity in computer time) and required a large amount of free memory. (McCarthy et al 1960) As computers became faster and garbage collection algorithms became more sophisticated, this cost became less onerous. By 2005, a UMass study found that a certain garbage collector could achieve 69% of the speed of manual memory management while using 2.1x as much memory, (Hertz & Berger 2005) which after decades of exponential computer performance growth, was good enough for many applications. In 1989, 1994, and 1999, the memory-unsafe programming languages C and C++, which use manual memory management, held the #1 and #2 spots in the TIOBE index of programming language popularity, but in 2004 and 2009 the garbage-collected language Java took first place. (TIOBE 2024)

There still is one area where garbage collection lags far behind manual memory management: latency, or the time taken to respond to an event like a message from another computer or a reading from an external sensor. Garbage collectors must pause a program’s execution at least part of the time while they are scanning through its memory. A paper on Java’s new high-performance garbage collector ZGC touts its 1.4 millisecond maximum pause times, in contrast to its predecessor G1, which could pause execution for up to 250 ms. (Yang & Wrigstad 2022). However the Linux kernel with the PREEMPT_RT patch, which adds some modifications to lower latency, can respond to events in just 0.16 milliseconds! (Adam 2021) Some users rely on these fast response times, so for projects like Linux, garbage collection is not a good fit.

Fortunately, it is possible to create a temporally memory safe programming language without using a garbage collector. Programming language researchers had been looking into how this could be achieved for decades, but the first such language to achieve widespread use was Rust. Engineers at browser company Mozilla began creating Rust in 2006. The language reached version 1 in 2015 and is now being used by Dropbox, Amazon Web Services, and Microsoft (Thompson 2023). Rust uses a concept called “ownership” to ensure temporal memory safety. Each memory object is “owned” by one and only one reference. Other references can “borrow” the object and refer to it without owning it, but they are required to always cease to exist before the reference that owns the object does, effectively “giving back” the object to its owner. Ownership can also be permenantly transferred to a different reference at which point the original owner reference becomes invalid and the programmer is not allowed to use it. Because an object’s owner reference will last longer than every other reference to that object, when the owner reference is deleted there will be no references to the object and it will always be safe to delete the object from memory (Klabnik and Nichols 2024). Thus temporal safety is achieved.

The Movement Towards Memory Safety

The cost of security vulnerabilities is growing. After the previously mentioned WannaCry virus, another virus exploiting the same bug known as NotPetya, which US officials claim was created by the Russian military to wage cyberwar on Ukraine, caused an estimated $10 billion dollars in damages. (Greenberg 2018). This brought the total estimated cost of the single Windows vulnerability up to a staggering $14 billion. Total ransomware payments jumped from $220 million in 2019 to $905 million in 2020, then crept up to a 2023 total of $1.1 billion, according to crypto security firm Chainanalysis (Chainanalysis Team 2024).

Faced with this crisis, the computer industry has decided to tackle one of the root causes of security vulnerabilities by moving towards memory safe languages. Starting in 2019, Google’s Android team started switching toward memory safe languages like Rust for new code and as a result, the number of Critical Severity vulnerabilities found in Android per year dropped by more than 50% in 3 years (Vander Stroep 2024). Mozilla has converted many components of its Firefox browser to Rust, including its rendering engine and CSS processor. (MozillaWiki, n.d.) In 2023, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency published guidance that “urges software manufacturers to make it a top-level company goal to reduce and eventually eliminate memory safety vulnerabilities from their product lines,” citing the Rust language as an important means of doing so. (Lord 2023) If these efforts succeed, memory safety bugs could finally be a thing of the past.
